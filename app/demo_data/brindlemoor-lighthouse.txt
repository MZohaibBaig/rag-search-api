Brindlemoor Lighthouse: A Fact Sheet

Overview
Brindlemoor Lighthouse stands on Gannet Rock, a basalt outcrop roughly four kilometres off the northern coast of the fictional island of Skerrimay. The tower is 38 metres tall, built of grey granite blocks, and painted with a single broad red band around its midpoint. Its light can be seen from 29 kilometres away in clear weather. It has guided fishing boats and cargo ships past the Gannet Reefs since it first shone on 14 October 1874.

History and Construction
The Skerrimay Harbour Board commissioned the lighthouse after the steamship Halcyon Dawn struck the Gannet Reefs in the winter of 1871, with the loss of 23 crew. The engineer Ottoline Varga designed the tower and supervised its construction. Building took three years because work was only possible between April and September, when the sea around Gannet Rock was calm enough to land stone. In total, 4,100 granite blocks were quarried at Pellow Cove and ferried out by a small paddle steamer named the Tern. The final cost was 61,000 pounds, which was almost double the original estimate.

The Lamp and Optics
The original light was a paraffin lamp behind a first-order Fresnel lens made in Paris by a workshop that shipped 1,008 individual glass prisms in numbered crates. The lens assembly weighs about 2.4 tonnes and floats on a bath of liquid mercury, so that the whole thing can rotate with a single push of a finger. It completes one full rotation every 20 seconds, and the light flashes white twice in quick succession every 20 seconds. This two-flash pattern is what identifies Brindlemoor to sailors; no other light on the Skerrimay coast uses it.

The paraffin lamp was replaced by an electric lamp in 1958, when a diesel generator was installed in the base of the tower. In 1994 the generator was retired and the light was converted to run on solar panels and a battery bank. The lamp today is a 1,000-watt halogen bulb. The original Fresnel lens is still in use, which makes Brindlemoor one of only nine lighthouses in the region that still use their first-generation glass.

The Keepers
From 1874 until automation in 1989, Brindlemoor was staffed by three keepers at a time, who worked in rotation: two on the rock and one on shore leave. Each keeper's shift pattern was four weeks on the rock followed by two weeks ashore. The first head keeper was Silas Threlfall, who served for 22 years and kept a daily weather log that is now held by the Skerrimay Maritime Museum. The most famous keeper was Ines Okonkwo-Ward, who in the storm of January 1953 kept the lamp lit for 71 hours without relief after the relief boat could not reach the rock. She was awarded the Harbour Board's silver medal for the effort.

Living conditions on the rock were hard. Fresh water was collected from the tower roof in a rainwater cistern that held 9,000 litres. Food was delivered fortnightly by boat when the weather allowed; in winter the keepers regularly went a month without a delivery and kept hens and a small vegetable box in a walled yard at the base of the tower. Keepers were forbidden to leave the rock except in an emergency. The lighthouse was automated on 30 June 1989, and the last keeper, Dov Abramsen, left the rock that afternoon.

Tides, Weather and Hazards
Gannet Rock is exposed to the open sea on three sides. Spring tides around the rock reach a range of 6.2 metres, and landing is only practical for about two hours either side of low water. The Gannet Reefs extend 1.5 kilometres to the south-west of the tower and are covered at high tide, which is the main reason ships need the light. Fog is most common in May and June, and the tower has a fog signal: a compressed-air horn that sounds one long blast every 30 seconds when visibility drops below two kilometres. The signal was upgraded from an explosive-charge system in 1932.

Wildlife
Gannet Rock takes its name from the northern gannets that nest on it, with a colony of roughly 3,000 pairs at its peak in summer. Grey seals haul out on the eastern ledges throughout the year. Because of the nesting birds, access to the rock is restricted between March and August each year, and no one may land without a permit from the Skerrimay Wildlife Trust.

Visiting
The lighthouse is not open for daily public tours. The Skerrimay Maritime Museum runs eight guided boat trips to the rock each year, all in September and October when the birds have left, and each trip carries at most 12 passengers. Tickets go on sale in June and normally sell out within a day. Visitors climb the 176 steps of the spiral staircase to the lamp room, and are asked to wear flat shoes with grip. Children under 10 are not permitted to make the landing. The Brindlemoor Lighthouse Trust, a registered charity formed in 2003, owns the tower and pays for its maintenance through membership fees and the boat trips.
